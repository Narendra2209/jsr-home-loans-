Balance transfer · 10 min read

A balance transfer means one thing: a new lender pays off your existing home loan and you continue with them at a lower rate. It is one of the few genuinely free lunches in personal finance — and one of the most commonly mishandled, because the arithmetic that decides whether it is worth doing is not the arithmetic most people run.

The test, in three lines

A transfer is worth doing when all three of these hold:

  1. The rate gap is 0.4 percentage points or more.
  2. You have more than about 7 years of tenure remaining.
  3. The saving repays the switching cost within roughly 18 months.

The middle condition is the one people miss. In the first years of a loan almost every rupee of the EMI is interest, so a rate cut removes a lot of it. In the last years, the EMI is mostly principal and a lower rate has very little left to work on. Transferring a loan with three years to run is usually not worth the paperwork, however attractive the rate looks.

Before you transfer: the call your bank hopes you will not make

Your floating rate is the external benchmark plus a spread that was fixed for you on the day you signed. The benchmark has moved for everybody; your spread has not. A borrower walking in today may be offered a thinner spread on the same product — which is precisely why your five-year-old loan has drifted above the market.

Most lenders will reset an existing borrower’s spread to the current card rate for a one-time conversion or switch fee, usually a few thousand rupees or a small fraction of the outstanding. They do not advertise it. Ask for it in writing before you start a transfer:

“My loan account is currently at X%. Your published rate for a new borrower with my profile is Y%. Please confirm the conversion fee to reset my spread to the current rate, and the effective date.”

If they agree, you get most of the benefit for a fraction of the cost and none of the paperwork. If they refuse, you have a competing quote in hand and the transfer becomes straightforward. Either way you win — and very often the formal transfer application is itself what produces the better internal offer.

Lower the EMI or shorten the tenure?

When your rate drops, the new lender will normally keep the tenure and reduce the EMI, because a lower EMI is easier to sell. If your cash flow is comfortable, ask them to do the opposite: keep the EMI where it is and shorten the tenure.

On a ₹40 lakh balance with 15 years left, moving from 9.4% to 8.5% saves about ₹2,140 a month if you take the lower EMI. Keep the old EMI instead, and you finish about a year and a half early, saving materially more interest. Same rate, same loan, considerably better outcome — entirely because of which box was ticked.

The top-up question

Most lenders will offer a top-up loan alongside the transfer — extra money over and above the balance being taken over, priced close to the home loan rate. For renovation, a child’s education or clearing high-cost debt, this is far cheaper than a personal loan, and it is one of the main reasons people transfer at all.

Two cautions. First, the combined amount must stay within the lender’s loan-to-value limit for the property. Second, a top-up used to consolidate credit card debt only works if the cards then stay clear — otherwise you have converted unsecured debt into debt secured on your home and kept the original problem.

The process, and how long each part takes

  1. Get a foreclosure quote and a list of documents from your existing lender. They must tell you the exact amount to close the loan and list every original property document they hold. 2–5 days.
  2. Apply to the new lender. Fresh KYC, income documents and the existing loan’s statement and sanction letter. 1–2 days once you have the papers together.
  3. Credit appraisal and sanction. The new lender assesses you afresh. 3–7 days.
  4. Legal and technical on the property. Runs off photocopies since the originals are with your current lender. 3–7 days.
  5. Disbursement to the old lender. The new lender issues a cheque or transfer directly to your existing loan account. Never routed through you. 2–3 days.
  6. Document handover. Your old lender releases the original property papers to the new one. This is the step that most often slips — chase it. 7–15 days.
  7. Fresh mortgage registration and first EMI. The new mortgage is registered and your new EMI cycle starts.

Total: usually 15–30 days.

Documents to keep ready

  • KYC for every applicant — PAN, Aadhaar, address proof, photographs
  • Income documents: 3 months’ salary slips, Form 16 and ITR for 2 years, 6 months’ bank statements (salaried); 3 years’ ITR and audited financials, 12 months’ bank statements (self-employed)
  • Existing loan sanction letter and the full repayment schedule
  • Loan account statement for the last 12 months — the new lender wants to see a clean repayment track
  • Foreclosure / outstanding balance letter from the existing lender
  • List of documents held by the existing lender, on their letterhead
  • Copies of the complete property document set, including the prior title chain
  • Latest property tax receipt and, where relevant, the occupancy certificate

Six mistakes that cost people money

  1. Transferring for a 0.15% gap. The saving will not cover the switching cost. Do the arithmetic first.
  2. Not asking the existing bank for a spread reset. The cheapest fix is nearly always the one you do not have to move for.
  3. Applying to four lenders at once. Four hard enquiries in a fortnight will damage the score you are relying on to get the better rate.
  4. Comparing the rate but not the fees. A 8.45% offer with a 1% processing fee can be worse than 8.55% with none, on a shorter remaining tenure.
  5. Taking the lower EMI by default. Keeping the EMI and cutting the tenure is worth considerably more.
  6. Not verifying the closure. Two things to check after it is done: that every original document has physically moved, and that the old loan shows as closed on your credit report within 45 days. An account left showing as live will affect your eligibility for years.

A note on foreclosure charges

On floating-rate home loans taken by individual borrowers, lenders are not permitted to levy a foreclosure or prepayment penalty. This is the rule that makes balance transfers viable at all. Fixed-rate loans are a different matter and can attract 2%–4%. Check which one you actually have — the sanction letter will say — before you assume the exit is free.
