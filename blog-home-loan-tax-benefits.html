Home loan tax benefits: old regime vs new regime

Home › Blog › Tax

Tax · 8 min read

For two decades, “buy a house, save tax” was standard advice in India. Then the new tax regime became the default, and most of those deductions stopped applying to most people. If you are still choosing a loan on the assumption that the interest is deductible, this is the page to read first.

Read this before anything else

Tax law changes with every Finance Act, and how these provisions apply depends on your specific facts — when the loan was sanctioned, whether the property is self-occupied or let out, whether construction is complete, and which regime you have opted for. This article is an orientation, not tax advice. Confirm your own position with a chartered accountant before you file.

The two deductions that matter

Under the old tax regime, a home loan on a self-occupied property gives you two separate deductions:

SectionCoversAnnual limit (self-occupied)
24(b)Interest paid on the home loan₹2,00,000
80CPrincipal repaid, plus stamp duty and registration in the year paid₹1,50,000 (shared with PF, ELSS, insurance, tuition fees and the rest of 80C)

Together that is up to ₹3.5 lakh of deduction a year — worth around ₹1.09 lakh in tax at a 30% slab, though the 80C limit is usually already partly consumed by EPF and insurance premiums, so the realistic incremental benefit is smaller than the headline.

What the new regime changed

The new regime under Section 115BAC has been the default since FY 2023-24. You have to actively opt out to use the old one. Under the new regime:

  • Section 80C is not available. No deduction for principal repayment, stamp duty or registration.
  • Section 24(b) is not available for a self-occupied property. This is the big one. If you live in the house, the interest deduction is gone.
  • Interest on a let-out property is still deductible against that property’s rental income — but a resulting loss from house property cannot be set off against your salary or other income under the new regime.

What this means in practice

If you are on the new regime and the property is self-occupied, the loan gives you no income tax benefit. That does not make the loan a bad idea — a home loan is still the cheapest borrowing available to a household — but it does mean you should stop treating the deduction as part of the return, and it means the old advice about not prepaying “because of the tax benefit” no longer holds.

Which regime is better for you depends on your total deductions, not on the home loan alone. Run both. If your combined 80C, 24(b), HRA, 80D and the rest add up to more than the new regime’s wider slabs and higher standard deduction give you, the old regime wins — otherwise it does not.

Section 24(b) in detail

  • Self-occupied: up to ₹2,00,000 of interest a year, old regime only.
  • Let out: the entire interest is deductible against rental income. However, the net loss from house property that can be set off against other heads is capped at ₹2,00,000 a year under the old regime, with the balance carried forward for up to 8 years to be set off against future house property income.
  • Completion condition: for a self-occupied property, the ₹2 lakh limit applies only if construction or acquisition is completed within 5 years from the end of the financial year in which the loan was taken. Miss that window and the limit drops to ₹30,000.
  • Pre-construction interest: interest paid before the property is completed is not lost. It is aggregated and claimed in five equal annual instalments starting from the year construction is completed — but it still has to fit inside the same ₹2 lakh annual cap for a self-occupied property.
  • Repairs or reconstruction: a loan taken for repairs to a self-occupied property carries a much smaller limit — ₹30,000.

Section 80C in detail

  • Only the principal portion of your EMI counts, not the interest.
  • Stamp duty and registration charges are allowed, but only in the financial year in which they are actually paid. For most buyers this is a one-time, and often substantial, claim — do not miss it.
  • The property must be completed. No 80C claim on principal repaid while the property is under construction.
  • Five-year lock-in: if you sell the house within five years from the end of the financial year in which you took possession, all the 80C deductions already claimed are added back to your income in the year of sale. This catches people out.
  • The ₹1.5 lakh ceiling is shared with EPF, PPF, ELSS, life insurance premiums, children’s tuition fees and the rest of 80C.

Sections 80EE and 80EEA

These gave first-time buyers an additional interest deduction over and above Section 24(b) — ₹50,000 under 80EE and ₹1.5 lakh under 80EEA for affordable housing. Both were time-bound and are closed to new loans: 80EEA applied to loans sanctioned up to 31 March 2022.

If your loan was sanctioned inside the eligible window and met the conditions — stamp duty value within the prescribed limit, no other house owned on the sanction date — you can continue to claim it until the loan is repaid, under the old regime. It is worth checking your sanction date; this is one of the most commonly missed deductions among borrowers who qualified.

Joint home loans: where the real money is

If a property is jointly owned and both owners are co-borrowers on the loan, each can claim the deductions separately, in proportion to their share of the ownership and repayment. Two co-owners on the old regime can therefore claim up to ₹4 lakh of interest and ₹3 lakh of principal between them, against ₹2 lakh and ₹1.5 lakh for a single owner.

Three conditions are non-negotiable, and all three are checked:

  1. Both people must be co-owners of the property, on the sale deed.
  2. Both must be co-borrowers on the loan, in the sanction letter.
  3. Each must actually pay their share of the EMI, traceable through their own bank account.

Adding a spouse to the deed after the loan is sanctioned, or paying the whole EMI from one account, will not support two claims. Get the structure right at sanction — it is very hard to fix afterwards.

The question everyone asks: should I prepay?

If you are on the new regime, there is no tax argument against prepaying at all. Prepay whenever you have surplus that is not earning more than your loan rate after tax.

If you are on the old regime and your interest is already above ₹2 lakh a year, the interest beyond that cap is giving you nothing back. Prepaying reduces exactly that unrewarded portion first, so the tax argument against prepayment is far weaker than it is usually made out to be.

And when you do prepay, ask the lender to reduce the tenure, not the EMI. That is where the saving actually comes from.

Documents you need at filing time

  • Provisional interest certificate from the lender, showing the principal and interest split for the year
  • Possession or completion certificate
  • Stamp duty and registration receipts, for the year in which you paid them
  • Sale deed showing co-ownership, if you are claiming jointly
  • Sanction letter, if you are claiming under 80EE or 80EEA

Your lender issues the interest certificate free, usually through net banking. Ask for it every year in April.
