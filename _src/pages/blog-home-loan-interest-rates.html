How home loan interest rates are actually set

Home › Blog › Interest rates

Interest rates · 9 min read

Most borrowers treat the interest rate as something handed to them, like a price tag. It is not. Roughly half of it is set by the Reserve Bank of India and applies to everyone equally; the other half is a number the lender chooses for you, and that half is negotiable. Knowing which is which is the whole game.

The two halves of your rate

Since October 2019, the RBI has required banks to link all new floating-rate retail loans — home loans included — to an external benchmark. Most banks use the RBI’s repo rate. So your rate looks like this:

Your rate = external benchmark (repo rate) + spread

The benchmark is identical for every borrower at every bank. When the RBI cuts the repo rate, every external-benchmark-linked loan in the country is repriced downward within a quarter.

The spread is where the differences live. It has two parts: a fixed operating-cost component the bank can normally only change once every three years, and a credit-risk premium specific to you — your credit score, employer, income stability, loan-to-value and whether the property is one the bank likes. Two people can walk into the same branch on the same day and be quoted rates 80 basis points apart entirely because of this.

Why your old loan drifted above the market

Your spread was fixed on the day you signed. A borrower sanctioned today, with the bank competing for new business, may be given a thinner one. The benchmark moved for you both; the spread did not. This is the single most common reason a five-year-old loan sits 60–100 basis points above what the same bank is quoting new customers — and the reason a phone call can fix it.

The vocabulary that appears in your sanction letter

Repo rate
The rate at which the RBI lends to commercial banks. The reference point for most floating home loans.
EBLR / RLLR
External Benchmark Lending Rate, or Repo Linked Lending Rate. The bank’s published floor rate: the benchmark plus its own base markup. Your rate is this plus your personal spread.
MCLR
Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate. The older, internal benchmark used for loans sanctioned between 2016 and 2019. MCLR-linked loans pass on RBI cuts more slowly. If you are still on one, ask to convert.
Reset date
How often your rate is refreshed against the benchmark — at least once every three months by regulation. Between resets, a repo cut does not reach you.
Spread / margin
The bank’s markup over the benchmark, fixed for you at sanction.
Key Facts Statement (KFS)
A standardised one-page summary lenders must give retail borrowers, showing the all-in annual percentage rate and every fee. Read it before the sanction letter — it is the only document designed to be comparable across lenders.

What decides the spread you are offered

  1. Credit score. The largest single lever. Most lenders publish a rate grid with bands at 800+, 750–799, 700–749 and below 700. Moving up one band is typically worth 10–50 basis points.
  2. Loan-to-value. Borrowing 60% of the property value gets a better rate than borrowing 85%. If you are close to a slab boundary, putting in a little more of your own money can pay for itself.
  3. Loan amount. Counter-intuitively, very small loans often carry higher rates — the fixed cost of processing has to be recovered somewhere.
  4. Employer category. Banks maintain internal lists. Government, PSU, listed companies and large MNCs sit in the top category; small unlisted firms and proprietorships are priced higher.
  5. Salaried vs self-employed. Self-employed applicants usually pay 15–50 basis points more for the same score, because income is harder to verify.
  6. Woman applicant or co-owner. Several lenders offer a 5–10 basis point concession.
  7. The property itself. A flat in a lender-approved project is cheaper to fund than a resale independent house with a complicated title. Approved-project financing is faster and better priced.

Six ways to get a better rate on a new loan

  1. Fix your credit report before you apply, not after. Once a lender has pulled your report and priced you, renegotiating is much harder. Pull your own report first — see our CIBIL guide.
  2. Get two lenders competing, in writing. A verbal “we can match that” is worth nothing. A sanction letter from a rival bank is the only leverage that reliably works.
  3. Apply to two lenders, not six. Every application generates a hard enquiry. A cluster of enquiries in a fortnight pushes your score down at exactly the wrong moment.
  4. Ask for the rate without the insurance. A rate quoted “subject to” a single-premium protection policy funded into the loan is not that rate. Get both quotes and compare like with like.
  5. Negotiate the processing fee separately. It is discretionary far more often than the rate is, and full waivers during campaign periods are common.
  6. Time the application around campaigns. Festive-season offers between roughly September and January are real, not marketing — banks chase disbursement targets and fee waivers appear.

If you already have a loan: the request that costs almost nothing

You do not have to switch banks to get a better rate. Every lender has an internal process — variously called a spread reset, rate conversion or switch fee — that lets an existing borrower move to the spread currently offered to new customers, usually for a small one-time fee of a few thousand rupees or a fraction of a percent of the outstanding.

Banks do not advertise this. Here is how to use it:

  1. Find your current rate and your reset date on your latest statement or in the lender’s app.
  2. Look up the bank’s published card rate for a new borrower with your profile.
  3. If the gap is more than about 40 basis points, get a written quote from a competing lender.
  4. Email your relationship manager and the branch: ask for the current spread to be applied to your existing loan, state the competing offer, and ask what the conversion fee is.
  5. If the answer is no, run the numbers on a balance transfer. Very often the formal transfer application is what produces a better internal offer.

A conversion typically costs a few thousand rupees against a saving in the lakhs. It is the highest return-on-effort action available to most home loan borrowers, and it takes one email.

Worth knowing

When your floating rate is reset upward, lenders are required to communicate the impact and give you options — increase the EMI, extend the tenure, or a combination — along with the option to switch to a fixed rate and the option to prepay. If your rate went up and you were quietly given a longer tenure instead, you are entitled to ask for that to be reworked.

Fixed or floating?

Almost all Indian home loans are floating, and that is usually right. Floating-rate home loans to individuals cannot carry a foreclosure or prepayment penalty, so you keep the freedom to prepay or move. Fixed-rate offers are generally fixed only for an initial period before reverting to floating, are priced higher to begin with, and can carry a prepayment charge of 2–4%.

The case for fixed is narrow: you are close to your affordability ceiling, the tenure is short, and certainty is worth more to you than the expected saving. For everyone else, floating with the discipline to prepay wins.

What this is worth in rupees

On a ₹50 lakh loan over 20 years, half a percentage point is about ₹1,600 a month and nearly ₹3.9 lakh over the tenure. A full percentage point is closer to ₹7.7 lakh. Set against an afternoon of phone calls and a conversion fee, there are few better uses of your time.
