How to improve your CIBIL score before you apply

Home › Blog › Credit score

Credit score · 8 min read

A credit score is not a judgement of your character. It is a statistical estimate of how likely you are to miss a payment in the next twelve months, built from a fairly small set of inputs. Once you know which inputs matter, moving the number is mechanical — and moving from 720 to 760 before you apply is worth real money on a home loan.

The bands lenders actually use

ScoreWhat it means for a home loan
800–900Best available pricing. You have room to negotiate on fees as well.
750–799Approved comfortably at or near card rate. This is the band to aim for.
700–749Usually approved, priced 10–50 basis points higher. Worth fixing before you apply.
650–699Case by case. Expect a higher rate, a lower loan-to-value, or a demand for a co-applicant.
Below 650Difficult with mainstream banks. Some housing finance companies will look, at a materially higher rate.
-1 or NH/NANo credit history. Not a bad score — but you will be assessed on income and banking alone.

India has four credit bureaus — TransUnion CIBIL, Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark. Lenders do not all use the same one, and your scores will differ between them, sometimes by 30–40 points. Check at least CIBIL and one other.

What the score is made of

The exact weightings are proprietary, but the broad shape is consistent across bureaus:

  • Repayment history — roughly a third. Have you paid on time, every time? A single payment 30 days past due can cost 50–80 points and stays visible for years.
  • Credit utilisation — roughly a quarter to a third. How much of your available credit card limit you are using. This is the fastest thing to fix.
  • Length and depth of history — roughly a fifth. Older accounts help. A healthy mix of secured and unsecured credit helps a little.
  • Recent enquiries and new accounts — roughly a tenth. Several hard enquiries in a short window read as distress borrowing.

The seven things that actually work

  1. Get utilisation below 30%, ideally below 10%. If your card limit is ₹2 lakh, keep the reported balance under ₹60,000. The bureau sees the balance on your statement date, not after you pay — so paying the card down a few days before the statement generates the number the lender sees. This one change can move a score 20–40 points within two cycles.
  2. Never miss a due date again, on anything. Set auto-debit for at least the minimum on every card and loan. The minimum protects the score; the interest cost of carrying a balance is a separate problem to solve.
  3. Ask for a limit increase and do not use it. A higher limit with the same spending mechanically lowers utilisation. Ask for an increase without a hard pull — most issuers offer pre-approved increases.
  4. Do not close your oldest credit card. Closing it shortens your credit history and removes its limit from the utilisation calculation. Keep it alive with a small recurring charge.
  5. Clear small overdue balances completely. A forgotten ₹800 on an old card reported as overdue does the same damage as ₹80,000. Overdue amounts are the first thing a credit manager looks for.
  6. Never accept a “settlement”. Settling a debt for less than the full amount marks the account as settled or written off, which is far worse for a home loan than a few late payments. If you can, pay the full outstanding and get a closed status plus a no-dues certificate in writing.
  7. Stop applying. No new cards, no consumer-durable loans, no “check your eligibility” forms in the six months before a home loan application. Each hard enquiry costs a few points and the cluster costs more.

What does not work

  • Paying anyone to “fix” your score. Nobody can delete accurate information from a credit report. Services that claim otherwise are selling you the dispute process you can run yourself for free.
  • Checking your own score. A self-check is a soft enquiry and has no effect. Check it monthly if you like.
  • Closing all your cards. A thin file is not the same as a clean one. Lenders want to see credit used responsibly, not credit avoided.
  • Keeping a rolling balance to “build history”. A myth. Pay in full; the account still reports as active and on time.

Check your report for errors — there is a good chance there is one

You are entitled to one free full credit report per calendar year from each of the four bureaus. Reporting errors are common: a loan you closed still showing as live, an account that is not yours, a settled status applied wrongly, an address or PAN mismatch merging two people’s files.

To dispute one:

  1. Download the full report, not just the score, from each bureau’s website.
  2. Raise a dispute online with the bureau, attaching your no-dues certificate, closure letter or bank statement.
  3. Simultaneously write to the lender that reported the entry — the bureau can only correct what the lender confirms.
  4. Bureaus and lenders are required to resolve credit information disputes within 30 days, and a compensation framework applies for delays beyond that. Keep a written record of when you raised it.

Since credit institutions now report to the bureaus fortnightly rather than monthly, a correction or a paid-off balance shows up considerably faster than it used to — usually within two to four weeks rather than two months.

A realistic 90-day plan

Days 1–7: pull all four reports. List every account, every overdue amount, every error.

Days 7–14: raise disputes on errors. Clear every overdue balance, smallest first. Set auto-debits.

Days 14–45: bring card utilisation under 10% before each statement date. Request limit increases. Apply for nothing.

Days 45–90: keep everything on time, check that disputes have been actioned, and re-pull your report. A file whose only problem was utilisation and a stale error typically gains 30–60 points over this period. A file with a genuine default will take longer — there is no shortcut for that.

If your score will not recover in time

You still have options, and they are worth more than waiting a year:

  • Add a co-applicant with a strong score. Lenders generally price on the primary applicant, and a spouse or parent with a clean file can change the outcome entirely.
  • Lower the loan-to-value. Putting in more of your own money reduces the lender’s risk and often gets a marginal file through.
  • Choose the right lender. Housing finance companies and some private banks assess weak-score, strong-income files that public sector banks decline outright. The rate is higher — and you can transfer out after 12–18 months of clean repayment, which is a perfectly good plan.

Before you act on this

Scoring models, bureau processes and reporting rules change over time, and lenders apply their own overlays on top of the score. Treat this as a guide to the mechanics rather than a guarantee of outcome, and confirm current rules with the bureau concerned.
